The army

Meet the pieces

Each side starts with sixteen: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights and eight pawns. Point values are a rough guide to relative strength, not a rule.

♚︎

King Priceless

Moves one square in any direction. It can never step onto a square that an enemy piece attacks, and losing it means losing the game.

Tuck it away early. In the middlegame it is a target. In the endgame it becomes a fighter.

♛︎

Queen 9 points

Slides any number of squares along a rank, file or diagonal. The most powerful piece on the board.

Bring it out too early and the minor pieces will chase it around while you lose time.

♜︎

Rook 5 points

Slides any number of squares along a rank or file. Takes part in castling.

Rooks love open files and the seventh rank, and they become strong once the board opens up.

♝︎

Bishop 3 points

Slides along diagonals and stays on one colour of square for the whole game.

Two bishops working together cover both colours and are strong in open positions.

♞︎

Knight 3 points

Jumps in an L: two squares one way and one square at a right angle. It is the only piece that can leap over others.

Knights shine in crowded positions and on outposts that enemy pawns cannot attack.

♟︎

Pawn 1 point

Moves one square forward, or two from its starting square. Captures one square diagonally forward. Promotes when it reaches the far rank.

Pawns never move backward, so every pawn move is a commitment.

The laws

Rules of the game

Chess has a few special rules that surprise new players. Learn these nine and you can play a full game.

Setup

The board is 8 by 8. Each player has a light square in the right-hand corner. The queen starts on her own colour: the white queen on a light square, the black queen on a dark one. White always moves first.

Winning

You win by checkmate: the enemy king is attacked and cannot escape. You also win if your opponent resigns or runs out of time.

Check

A king under attack is in check, and the player must fix it at once. Move the king, block the attack, or capture the attacking piece. A move that leaves your own king in check is illegal.

Castling

The king moves two squares toward a rook, and the rook jumps to the other side of the king. It is allowed only if neither piece has moved, no pieces stand between them, the king is not in check, and the king does not cross or land on an attacked square.

En passant

If a pawn advances two squares and lands beside an enemy pawn, that pawn may capture it as if it had moved only one square. This is allowed on the very next move only.

Promotion

A pawn that reaches the far rank becomes a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same colour. Most players choose a queen, but choosing a knight is sometimes the winning idea.

Touch-move

In over-the-board play, if you touch a piece you must move it, and if you touch an enemy piece you must capture it when that is legal. To adjust a piece you say "j'adoube" first.

Draws

A game is drawn by stalemate (no legal move and no check), by threefold repetition of a position, by fifty moves by each side with no capture or pawn move, when neither side has enough material to mate, or by agreement.

Time controls

Games are timed. Under FIDE's categories, bullet is under 3 minutes per player, blitz is 3 to 10, rapid is 10 to 60, and classical is longer than that.

Moves are written in algebraic notation: files a to h, ranks 1 to 8. "Nf3" means a knight moves to f3, "x" means capture, "+" check, "#" checkmate, and "O-O" castling.

Opening theory

Gambits

A gambit is an opening where a player gives up material, usually a pawn, to get faster development, more space or the initiative. The other side may accept the offer or decline it.

Positional

Queen's Gambit

1.d4 d5 2.c4

White offers the c-pawn to pull Black's d-pawn away from the centre. If Black accepts, White usually wins the pawn back, so it is a gambit mostly in name. It is among the most respected openings at every level, and the 2020 TV series of the same name brought it to a wide new audience.

Sharp attack

King's Gambit

1.e4 e5 2.f4

White gives the f-pawn for open lines against Black's king and a strong centre. It was the romantic favourite of the 19th century, and world champion Boris Spassky kept it alive in top-level games.

Attacking

Evans Gambit

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.b4

White sacrifices a wing pawn so that c3 and d4 can follow with tempo. Garry Kasparov brought it back to the top level when he played it against Viswanathan Anand in 1995.

Practical

Smith-Morra Gambit

1.e4 c5 2.d4 cxd4 3.c3

A popular weapon against the Sicilian Defence. White trades a pawn for quick development and open lines, and can often play on instinct while Black has to find accurate moves.

Sharp attack

Danish Gambit

1.e4 e5 2.d4 exd4 3.c3

White may give up two pawns to get both bishops pointing at Black's kingside. It is fast and fun, and Black has to know the safe path through the complications.

Counter-gambit

Budapest Gambit

1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5

Black answers White's queen-pawn opening by offering a pawn at once. After 3.dxe5 Ng4 Black hunts for the pawn back with active pieces and a few well-known traps.

Trappy

Englund Gambit

1.d4 e5

Black sacrifices a pawn on the very first move. It has some famous traps for careless opponents, but it is risky against accurate play.

Long-term pressure

Benko Gambit

1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 c5 3.d5 b5

Black gives a pawn on the queenside for open files and diagonals that pressure White for the rest of the game, even in the endgame.

Tactics

Tricks and tactics

Most games below master level are decided by tactics: short sequences that win material or checkmate. Learn the patterns and you will start seeing them everywhere.

Fork

One piece attacks two or more targets at the same time. Knights are the classic forkers because enemy pieces seldom see them coming. A knight fork on a king and queen wins the queen.

Pin

A piece cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it. If the piece behind is the king, the pinned piece is not allowed to move at all.

Skewer

The reverse of a pin. A valuable piece is attacked and must move away, which exposes a less valuable piece on the same line.

Discovered attack

Move one piece to uncover an attack by another. When the uncovered attack is a check, the moving piece can also do something else, and the opponent may lose a lot.

Double check

Two pieces give check at once. Blocking or capturing cannot answer both, so the king has to move.

Back-rank mate

A king stuck behind its own pawns on the first rank is mated by a rook or queen. Giving the king an escape square with a pawn move, called "luft", prevents it.

Deflection and decoy

Deflection forces a defender away from the square or piece it guards. A decoy lures an enemy piece onto a square where it can be attacked or where it blocks its own side.

Zwischenzug

German for "in-between move". Instead of making the expected recapture, you insert a stronger move first, often a check, and the position changes in your favour.

Smothered mate

A knight checks a king that is surrounded by its own pieces. It often follows a queen sacrifice that forces the king into the corner.

Play it through

Three famous traps

Pick a game and step through it. Each move is highlighted on the board. You can also tap any move in the list.

Before every move

Ask three questions: what checks can my opponent make, what captures, and what threats? Then look at the same three for yourself. This simple habit stops most blunders.

Opening habits

Fight for the centre, develop your knights and bishops, castle early, and avoid moving the same piece twice or bringing the queen out too soon.

Train daily

Solving ten puzzles a day builds pattern recognition faster than playing endless games. Every site in the list below has a puzzle section.

Hall of fame

Famous players

From the first official champion to today's young stars, these are names every chess player should know.

1837 to 1884 · USA

Paul Morphy

Toured Europe in the 1850s and beat the best players of his day with fast development and daring attacks. His Opera Game from 1858 is still taught to beginners.

1836 to 1900 · Austria

Wilhelm Steinitz

The first official World Champion, crowned in 1886. He taught that small advantages build up and should be played for patiently.

1888 to 1942 · Cuba

José Raúl Capablanca

World Champion from 1921 to 1927. Known for smooth, clear technique, especially in endgames.

1936 to 1992 · Latvia

Mikhail Tal

The Magician from Riga. He became World Champion in 1960 at 23, thrilling crowds with wild sacrifices.

1943 to 2008 · USA

Bobby Fischer

Beat Boris Spassky in Reykjavik in 1972 to become World Champion, ending a long run of Soviet title holders.

Born 1951 · Russia

Anatoly Karpov

World Champion from 1975 to 1985. He squeezed opponents with quiet positional play and won a huge number of tournaments.

Born 1963 · Russia

Garry Kasparov

World Champion from 1985 to 2000. His 1997 match loss to IBM's Deep Blue was a landmark moment for computers in chess.

Born 1969 · India

Viswanathan Anand

Five-time World Champion and India's first grandmaster, in 1988. He inspired a whole generation of Indian players.

Born 1976 · Hungary

Judit Polgár

The strongest woman player in history. She reached world number 8 and beat Garry Kasparov in 2002.

Born 1990 · Norway

Magnus Carlsen

World Champion from 2013 to 2023. He holds the highest rating ever recorded, 2882.

Born 2006 · India

D. Gukesh

Became the youngest undisputed World Champion in 2024, at age 18.

Born 2005 · India

R. Praggnanandhaa

Reached the final of the 2023 World Cup, where he lost to Magnus Carlsen in the tiebreaks.

Timeline

A short history

Chess is about fifteen centuries old and has changed a lot on the way.

  • c. 6th century

    Chaturanga, an Indian game with the four divisions of an army, appears. It is the ancestor of chess.

  • Later centuries

    The game spreads through Persia as shatranj, then across the Islamic world and into Europe.

  • c. 1475

    The queen and bishop gain their modern long-range moves in southern Europe. The game becomes much faster and looks like today's chess.

  • 1851

    The first international tournament takes place in London, and Adolf Anderssen wins.

  • 1886

    Wilhelm Steinitz becomes the first official World Champion.

  • 1924

    FIDE, the international chess federation, is founded in Paris.

  • 1972

    Bobby Fischer defeats Boris Spassky in Reykjavik.

  • 1997

    IBM's Deep Blue beats Garry Kasparov in a six-game match.

  • 2024

    D. Gukesh becomes the youngest undisputed World Champion, at age 18.
